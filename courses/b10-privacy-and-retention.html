Privacy and retention: metadata at one hundred percent, content on a sample

The last session, and the one where the course's own thesis meets its limit. Poor coverage hides failures. Rich content capture creates legal and security exposure. These pull in opposite directions and the resolution is not a compromise, it is a split: metadata on everything, content on a small consented sample with a short life. This session builds that split, prices the honesty of the default, and closes the course.

Nine sessions arguing for more telemetry. One session on the cost of that argument

Everything in this course has pushed one direction: instrument more, keep the attributes, log the tool arguments, capture what happened because you cannot replay it. This session is where that push meets data-protection law, security review, and a genuine trade-off that nobody gets to dodge. Part 1 · the tension

One vendor, two published positions, and the split between them 7 min live

You could argue this trade-off from first principles for an afternoon. You do not have to, because the same organisation published both halves of it within a year: the cost of protecting content, and the pattern that works anyway. Read them next to each other. That pair is this session.

LiveWhat the metadata-only default actually costs you4 min▶

Most writing on this topic sells the privacy-preserving default as free. It is not free, and pretending otherwise is how you lose the argument with the engineer who has to debug at 03:00. Be precise about what you keep and what you give up.

Metadata only, the defaultWith sampled content
Where did it fail?Answerable. Spans, durations, statuses and counts localise the failing step exactlyAnswerable, identically
Was retrieval empty?Answerable. Document count, ids and scores are metadataAnswerable, plus you can read what came back
Did it loop or truncate?Answerable. Step counts, finish reasons, token countsAnswerable, identically
Why did the model do that?Frequently not answerable. This is the real cost and it is the expensive half of debuggingAnswerable on the sampled fraction, which is usually enough
Legal exposureLow. Nothing personal left your boundaryReal, bounded, and manageable if it is consented, scoped and time-boxed

Which is why the course's simulator ends the way it does. Switching on full content capture as the final lever moves retention from 4.6 GB to 48.1 GB, a 10.5x increase, flips the PII flag to true, and leaves the diagnosis score unchanged at 5 of 5, because the message content was never what was broken. Note the honest boundary of that lesson: it holds for this incident, which was a swallowed timeout and a stale cache. For a prompt-injection incident or a subtle instruction-following regression, content is the diagnosis. That is the case the sample exists to serve.

Part 2 · what the spec says

Six sensitive attributes, three patterns, and one inverted default 7 min live

The specification has already made most of these decisions for you, and it made them in the safe direction. The verbatim default: "OpenTelemetry instrumentations SHOULD NOT capture them by default, but SHOULD provide an option for users to opt in." Everything below is the shape of that opt-in.

Content lives in three Opt-In attributes, and three more are flagged sensitive. All six matter, and the second group is the one teams forget:

AttributeStatusWhat is actually in it
gen_ai.input.messagesOpt-InThe user's words. Structured, with a mandatory JSON schema
gen_ai.output.messagesOpt-InThe model's words, including anything it repeated back from the input
gen_ai.system_instructionsOpt-InYour prompt engineering. Rarely personal data, frequently a trade secret
gen_ai.retrieval.query.textSensitiveWhat was searched for, which is often more revealing than the answer
gen_ai.tool.call.argumentsOpt-InThe account number. The email address. The date of birth you passed to a lookup
gen_ai.tool.call.resultOpt-InThe row that came back out of your own database
Redacting only the prompt is insufficient, and this is the most common mistake on this page A team switches off gen_ai.input.messages, declares the trace store clean, and passes review. Meanwhile gen_ai.tool.call.arguments is still on, because it is the field that makes tool debugging possible, and the tool arguments are frequently where the account number is. The user asked a vague question in natural language; your agent turned it into lookup_customer(nric="S1234567A"). The structured, machine-readable, easily-indexed version of the personal data is on the tool span, not on the prompt.
Self-studyThe env-var nuance, precisely2 min read▶

OTEL_INSTRUMENTATION_GENAI_CAPTURE_MESSAGE_CONTENT is the de-facto name of the opt-in switch, and you will find it in most instrumentation libraries. Be careful how you say that in a design review, because the spec mentions it only as "for example". What is mandated is that an opt-in gate exists and defaults to off. The name is a convention that libraries happen to share.

The practical consequence: never assume a library is safe because you did not set that variable. Two failure modes follow directly.

  • An instrumentation may honour a differently named flag, or a constructor argument, or a config file key. Grep for what the library reads rather than trusting the name.
  • Handling of streaming chunks is a bare TODO in the spec, so the behaviour of a streaming instrumentation with content capture is not specified. Test it rather than reasoning about it.

Verification is one span, not one document: run one real call through your pipeline in a staging environment and read the raw exported span. If prompt text, tool arguments or retrieval query text appear in it, your gate is not where you think it is.

Self-studyThe inverted default: OpenInference captures content unless you stop it3 min read▶

This is the single most consequential difference between the two dialects, and it is a privacy posture rather than a technical one. Both emit valid OTLP; every OpenInference trace is a valid OTLP trace. But:

  • OTel GenAI: content capture is Opt-In and off by default. You must act to expose data.
  • OpenInference: content is captured on by default. You hide it with the OPENINFERENCE_HIDE_* flag family, and every one of those flags defaults to False. You must act to protect data.

Two details worth knowing because they show up in review. OpenInference redaction replaces the value with the literal string "__REDACTED__", which is genuinely useful: the field's presence is preserved, so you can still see that there was a prompt and that it was hidden, rather than wondering whether instrumentation was broken. And OPENINFERENCE_BLOB_UPLOADER offloads oversized media, which is the same idea as the OTel upload hook except that it is implemented rather than being a TODO.

The lesson generalises past these two libraries: read the default of every instrumentation you install, and assume nothing from the family it belongs to. If you migrate a dialect, or add one framework instrumented by a different project, your privacy posture can invert with no change to your own code.

Part 3 · the pipeline

Scrub before ingestion, not after 6 min live

Where you redact matters more than how well you redact. Once content reaches a vendor endpoint you have made a cross-border transfer and created a processor relationship, and deletion becomes a request rather than a fact. Everything downstream of that line is somebody else's promise. So the whole design question is: how much can you strip while keeping the trace useful, and can you do it before the exporter.

Why allow-list beats deny-list, in one sentence you can use in a review A deny-list of PII patterns is a bet that you enumerated every shape personal data can take in free text written by strangers. You did not. Regular expressions catch the credit card and miss "my wife Sarah at the Tampines branch, the one who called on Tuesday". An allow-list inverts the failure mode: when you meet a field you did not anticipate, the safe thing happens by default and you lose a little diagnostic detail instead of leaking a person.
Retention and the law

Real numbers, and three principles that bite a trace store 8 min live

Retention policy is usually written as a document and then quietly overridden by a technical detail nobody surfaced. Below are the published numbers from one provider, and then the three data-protection principles that apply to your trace store whether or not anyone has told your team they do.

SurfaceWhat the vendor documents [P]What it means for your design
Conversation contentPrompts and outputs are not retained by defaultThe provider's default is already the good one. Your own trace store is usually the leak, not theirs
Zero Data RetentionExists per organisation, but does not cover code execution, Claude for Excel, or Teams and Enterprise interfacesZDR is not a property of your account, it is a property of a path. Enumerate your paths
CORS under ZDRUnsupportedA browser app must go through a backend proxy. This is an architecture decision disguised as a retention setting
Covered ModelsClaude Fable 5 and Mythos 5 require 30-day retention, are ZDR-ineligible, and a ZDR org calling them receives 400 invalid_request_errorYour retention policy can be set by your model choice, not by your policy document. A model swap is a compliance change
Compliance surfacesActivity Feed and remote session transcripts run to 6 yearsAsk which of your surfaces are compliance surfaces before you promise anyone a 30-day deletion window
The sentence to take to your next architecture review Your retention policy can be set by your model choice rather than by your policy document. A team with a written 7-day retention rule, a ZDR agreement and a signed DPIA can breach all three by merging a one-line config change that switches to a Covered Model. The only defence is a test: assert in CI that the model ids in production configuration are on your approved list, and fail the build otherwise.
GDPR Art. 5(1) [P]How it applies to a trace store
(c) Data minimisationCapturing full prompt text on 100% of traffic "in case we need to debug" is the textbook violation. So sampling and redaction are the compliance mechanism, not merely a cost mechanism. That reframing is what gets the work funded
(e) Storage limitationAn unbounded trace-retention default is non-compliant by construction. "We keep traces until the disk fills" is a policy, and it is the wrong one. Set a TTL per span category, with content on the shortest
(f) Integrity and confidentialityTraces hold prompts, tool arguments and retrieved documents in one place, usually in third-party SaaS, usually with broad internal read access. Trace stores deserve the access controls you give production databases, and most do not have them
Erasure requestsThey must reach the trace store. If you cannot delete one user's spans, you cannot comply. Which is why pseudonymous keys are an engineering requirement and not a nicety: you need a join key to find the rows
The PDPA trap, stated clearly Singapore's PDPC published Advisory Guidelines on the Use of Personal Data in AI Recommendation and Decision Systems on 1 March 2024. Two facts about them matter here. They are not legally binding, and per multiple independent law-firm summaries [S] they do not cover generative AI. So do not cite them as authority for LLM prompt-trace retention, which is a mistake that appears in a lot of internal decks. For an LLM application in Singapore the operative obligations are the PDPA's own: Consent, Purpose Limitation, Notification, Protection and Retention Limitation. Retention Limitation is the one that governs your trace store directly. Honest flag: the "excludes generative AI" scope statement is secondary [S] and should be re-verified against the PDF before you rely on it in writing.
Demo 1 of 2

Build the redaction layer for Beacon, before the exporter ★ 10 min · everyone builds

Beacon's Insight Bot answers executive questions about revenue, which means its tool arguments contain customer ids and its retrieved documents contain unreleased financials. Build the layer that lets the trace stay useful and stops any of that leaving the process.

Write the allow-list first, as data rather than as code. The starting set is the low-cardinality metadata: provider, model, operation, token counts, finish reasons, document counts and scores, plus your own tenant.id and feature.id. Everything not on the list is redacted. Review the list in a pull request like any other security artifact.

Redact structurally, not destructively. For every field you drop, keep its shape: length, token count, whether it parsed as valid JSON, and the ids and scores of anything retrieved. This is what keeps the trace diagnostic, and it is the part people skip because dropping the field is one line shorter.

Hash the identifiers with a keyed hash and a rotation plan, so you can still group by user and still cannot read the user. Note the tension you have just created and write it down: rotating the key breaks your ability to join across the rotation boundary, which is a cost worth paying and a surprise worth documenting.

Put it in the right place. A ReadableSpan is immutable by the time on_end is called, so a span processor cannot rewrite attributes: in production this belongs in an exporter wrapper or in the collector's own redaction and transform processors. The sketch below is deliberately the readable version rather than the correct one, and if you ship it you will get a very quiet no-op.

Verify with a real export. Send one live request through staging and read the raw exported payload. Grep it for a known test value that you planted in the prompt and in a tool argument. A redaction layer that has not been verified against a raw export is a belief, not a control.

★ Redaction sketch · allow-list, structural, keyed hash ALLOW = {"gen_ai.provider.name", "gen_ai.operation.name", "gen_ai.request.model", "gen_ai.response.model", "gen_ai.usage.input_tokens", "gen_ai.usage.output_tokens", "gen_ai.response.finish_reasons", "gen_ai.retrieval.documents.count", "tenant.id", "feature.id"} HASH = {"user.id", "gen_ai.conversation.id"} def scrub(attrs: dict) -> dict: out = {} for k, v in attrs.items(): if k in ALLOW: out[k] = v elif k in HASH: out[k] = hmac_hash(v) # joinable, unreadable elif isinstance(v, str): out[k + ".len"] = len(v) # keep the shape out[k + ".json_valid"] = is_json(v) out[k] = "__REDACTED__" # keep the field return out # NOTE: a ReadableSpan is immutable in on_end. In production this runs in an # exporter wrapper, or in the collector's redaction / transform processor.
Keep the field, not just the value Writing the literal "__REDACTED__" rather than deleting the key is the pattern OpenInference chose and it is worth copying. A missing attribute is ambiguous: it could mean redacted, or it could mean your instrumentation silently stopped emitting last Thursday. A present attribute with a redaction marker tells you the pipeline is alive and the policy is working, which is the difference between a control and a hope.
Demo 2 of 2

The erasure drill: delete one Beacon user from your traces ★ 10 min · everyone builds

This is a fire drill, and almost every team fails it the first time. One named Beacon user asks for their data to be erased. You have 30 days. Do it, with a clock running, and write down every place you had to look.

Find the rows. Given only a user identifier, list every span, event, metric and content blob attributable to that person. If your answer requires a full table scan of a SaaS trace store you may not be able to query that way, you have just discovered that your join key was missing rather than pseudonymous.

Follow the content out to the separate store. Pattern three keeps content externally with references on the spans, and the upload hook runs regardless of the sampling decision, which means content can exist for traces you never sampled. Deleting spans does not delete blobs.

Check whether you can actually delete. Some vendors offer trace deletion by id, some only by project or by TTL, and some only on request. Whatever you find, write the answer into your DPIA: "deletion is a support ticket with an unstated SLA" is a finding, and it is one your DPO needs before the request arrives, not after.

Now set the TTLs so most of this problem expires on its own. Metadata can live long because it is not personal. Content gets the shortest life you can defend, in a separate store, so that the majority of erasure requests are satisfied by a clock rather than by an engineer at a keyboard.

Finally, write the one-page policy: what is captured, at what rate, with whose consent, where it lives, who may read it, how long it survives, and how it is deleted. One page. Reviewed on a date. That document is the deliverable of this session, more than any code you wrote today.

The finding almost everyone gets Content lives in more places than the design diagram shows: the trace store, the blob store the upload hook wrote to, the eval dataset somebody exported to debug a regression, a CSV in a shared drive from an incident in March, and a Slack thread where an engineer pasted a failing prompt. Erasure has to reach all of them. The engineering lesson is not "be more careful with the CSV", it is capture less in the first place, because every copy you never made is a copy you never have to find.
Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 55-70 min total

  • Export one raw span from your production pipeline and read every attribute. Specifically look at gen_ai.tool.call.arguments and gen_ai.retrieval.query.text, not just the prompt. Most teams find personal data in a field they had never thought about.
  • For every instrumentation library you have installed, look up its content-capture default in the source rather than the README. If any of them captures by default, you have a live exposure and a one-line fix.
  • Run the erasure drill for one real user id and time it. Write the elapsed time and the list of places you had to look into your DPIA. That artifact is worth more to your DPO than any policy paragraph.
  • Check whether any model id in production configuration is on a retention-mandated Covered Model list, and add a CI assertion that fails the build if an unapproved model id appears in config.
  • Set an explicit TTL on every trace category you store, with content on the shortest. If any category currently has no TTL, that is an unbounded retention default, which Art. 5(1)(e) makes non-compliant by construction.
  • Write the one-page policy from Demo 2 and put a review date on it. Then diary the review. An alerting and retention policy nobody re-reads decays into exactly the configuration you had before you wrote it.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · A team switches off prompt and completion capture, declares the trace store free of personal data, and passes security review. What did they miss?

The user asked something vague in natural language; your agent turned it into a typed call with an identifier in it, and then a result row came back out of your own database. Both of those are Opt-In and sensitive in the spec, and both are usually left on because they are what makes tool debugging possible. Add the retrieval query text as a third: what someone searched for is often more revealing than the answer they got.

2 · Your organisation has a Zero Data Retention agreement and a written 7-day retention policy. An engineer merges a config change to a Covered Model. What happens?

This is the teaching point worth repeating out loud: your retention policy can be set by your model choice rather than by your policy document. Claude Fable 5 and Mythos 5 are documented as Covered Models requiring 30-day retention and are ZDR-ineligible. The defence is mechanical rather than procedural: assert in CI that every model id in production configuration is on your approved list, and fail the build otherwise. And note the sibling trap, that CORS is unsupported under ZDR, so a browser app must route through a backend proxy.

3 · You add one framework instrumented by OpenInference alongside your OTel GenAI stack, changing nothing else. What happens to your privacy posture?

Every OpenInference trace is a valid OTLP trace, and the dialects are genuinely interoperable, which is exactly what makes this trap easy to walk into. The defaults are opposite: OTel GenAI is opt-in and off, OpenInference is on and you opt out. So one added dependency can start shipping prompts to your trace store with no change to your own code. Generalise it into a habit: read the content-capture default of every instrumentation you install, in the source, and verify with one raw export.

Learn AI Observability with Phoebe · Leader track · Session 1 of 6

The trace is the receipt: "it works in evals" is not "it worked at 09:14"

An eval score is a claim about a population you constructed. A trace is the receipt for one request that one real person made at a specific minute on a specific Tuesday. This session is the argument for why your organisation needs the second thing even after it has bought the first, told through one incident inside a company and three that were published to the world. No code. Decisions, and the questions that surface them.

Part 0

The one sentence you are here to be able to say out loud

Evals tell you whether it works. Observability tells you what actually happened. Both are necessary, they cost different money, and they answer questions that cannot substitute for each other. The leader track exists because the second one is almost always the thing that gets cut, and the cut is invisible until the week it is not.

Part 1 · the incident

Tuesday, 09:14 10 min live

Beacon is a mid-size company with an internal assistant called Insight Bot. Executives ask it questions about the business and it answers in plain language over the warehouse. It shipped eleven weeks ago, it passed its evals comfortably, and it is well liked. This is the shape of the week it went wrong.

On Tuesday at 09:14, Insight Bot begins telling executives yesterday's revenue figure. The figure is wrong. Not wildly wrong, which is the problem - it is last Monday's number, delivered in this morning's sentence, in the same confident register as every correct answer it has ever given. It does this intermittently. It raises no error. It triggers no alert. The p95 latency dashboard is green all week, because the p95 latency genuinely is fine.

The first human signal arrives on Wednesday afternoon, when a finance lead notices a number in a slide deck she cannot reconcile. It is escalated as a data quality question, then as a warehouse question, then finally as an AI question. The mechanism is found on Thursday, by an engineer reading code rather than telemetry, because there was no telemetry to read.

LiveWhy the dashboard was green, and was not lying4 min▶

There is a temptation to treat a green dashboard during an incident as a monitoring failure, as though the tool broke. It did not. The dashboard aggregated exactly the signals it was given, correctly, and every one of those signals was genuinely healthy.

The signalWhat it reportedWhy that was correct
HTTP status200, every timeThe service did return a response. Success is not a status code
Latency p95Well inside targetServing a stale cache entry is fast. Degradation made it faster, not slower
Error rateFlatThe exception was caught, so from the framework's point of view no error occurred
Eval suitePassingIt runs against a fixed set of inputs and a fresh warehouse. It cannot see a Tuesday
User feedbackSilent for a dayThe answers were plausible. Nobody reports a plausible answer

The lesson to state plainly, because it is the whole reason this course exists: a monitoring system can only be wrong about things it measures. Beacon's failure lived entirely in the space between the measurements, and no amount of tuning the existing signals would have moved it. The fix was a decision about where to look, made by a person, before anybody knew what they were looking for.

Self-studyWhat Beacon tried first, and why each attempt failed3 min read▶

The order in which a team attacks an invisible failure tells you what it can see. Beacon's sequence is the common one, and every step in it was reasonable.

  • Re-run the evals. They passed, which was taken as evidence the assistant was fine. It was actually evidence that the eval harness has its own warehouse connection and its own fixed questions, and had never once exercised the fallback path.
  • Blame the model. A change of model was proposed within an hour of the first report. This is the most expensive wrong turn available, because it consumes a week and produces a confident negative result that means nothing.
  • Look at the prompt. Also reasonable, also useless. The prompt was correct. The model did exactly what a good model should do with the rows it was handed.
  • Ask the warehouse team. This got closest, because the timeout was real and visible in the warehouse's own logs. But nobody could connect a warehouse timeout to a specific assistant answer, because there was no shared identifier between the two systems. That join key is the thing a trace gives you for free.

Four days of senior time, spent proving what was not wrong. The cost of the missing instrumentation was not the incident; it was the investigation.

Part 2 · the elite case

What it looks like when it happens to the vendor 9 min live

The natural objection to Beacon is that Beacon was sloppy. So the most useful published artifact in this course is one where the team is not sloppy by any standard: Anthropic's own postmortem of three issues, September 2025, in which a model provider documents how hard its own degradation was to detect with its own instrumentation.

The four numbers, and what each one is for 16% of Sonnet 4 requests affected at peak in the worst hour is the severity. Approximately 30% of Claude Code users who made requests in that window had at least one message routed to the wrong server type - that is the same bug counted per person instead of per request, and it is always the larger and more frightening number. 0.18% on Bedrock and under 0.0004% on Vertex are the spread, and they carry the operational lesson: if you multi-home across platforms, one platform's telemetry does not represent the others, so a clean dashboard on your primary route is not evidence about your secondary one.
LiveThe sentence to read out loud in your next planning meeting4 min▶

The postmortem's explanation of the detection failure is the part worth quoting, because it dismantles the most common argument against production observability. The benchmarks, in their words, "simply didn't capture the degradation users were reporting".

Then it goes further and explains why, and the reason is genuinely counter-intuitive. Detection was hard partly because Claude recovers from isolated errors. A resilient system absorbs a fault and produces an acceptable answer anyway, which means an aggregate benchmark scored over many requests barely moves. Resilience and detectability pull against each other, and nobody puts that on a roadmap.

Add the third factor - the bugs produced different symptoms on different platforms at different rates - and you have the complete anatomy of a failure that a good offline suite cannot see:

  • It was intermittent, so any single test run could pass.
  • It was absorbed, so scored output degraded less than user experience did.
  • It was uneven across routes, so the population your benchmark samples was not the population being harmed.

Every one of those three is also true of Beacon. The difference in scale between a mid-size company and a frontier lab changed nothing about the shape of the problem, which is the strongest argument available that this is structural rather than a maturity gap you will grow out of.

Self-studyWhat the remediation was, and what it was not2 min read▶

Read the remediation section carefully, because the direction of travel is the point. The response to benchmarks missing a real degradation was not a smarter offline benchmark. It was more sensitive evaluation run continuously on real production traffic, plus better tooling for engineers to look at actual requests.

That is the a2 thesis arriving from an unexpected direction: when the best-resourced possible team discovers that offline testing cannot see a class of failure, it buys coverage on production rather than more offline testing. Session a2 turns that into a coverage decision you can actually make, and session a3 into dashboards that answer questions rather than reassure.

Part 3 · the legal case

When a silent failure becomes a finding of fact 8 min live

Beacon cost four days of senior time. The Anthropic issue cost reputation and a public postmortem. The third case is the one that changes how a general counsel hears this conversation, because it establishes that an AI answer is your company's statement, not your vendor's.

The instrument nobody has Read the row of green chips again. Every conventional signal was healthy, because the system did exactly what it was built to do: it produced a fluent answer, quickly, without erroring. The only signal that would have caught this is a comparison between what the assistant asserted and what your own documented policy says - and that signal does not exist in any observability product by default, because it requires you to have declared what your source of truth is. It is a coverage decision, and it is a2's subject.
Self-studyTwo more incidents you will hear cited, and how to cite them honestly3 min read▶

These two circulate constantly in AI risk decks. Both are useful and neither has a primary postmortem, so they belong in a footnote rather than a headline. Being the person in the room who knows the difference is worth more than the extra example.

Cursor / Anysphere support bot, April 2025  [S] secondary

The reported sequence is that the support assistant fabricated a single-device policy that did not exist, and users cancelled subscriptions before the company could intervene. No primary postmortem was found, so treat every detail as secondary reporting. The reason it is worth carrying anyway is the compound cause: a genuine infrastructure bug in session management, plus an agent confabulating an explanation for it. Your trace in that scenario shows a perfectly healthy agent and a sick auth service, and the assistant's confident wrong explanation is the part that reaches the customer.

Replit agent and a production database, July 2025  [S] secondary

An agent with production write access is reported to have destroyed data it should never have been able to touch. No primary postmortem was found, and every record count circulating for this incident is unverified - so do not quote one, including the one you have probably seen. Cite it for the control lesson only, which is that write scope is a design decision and not an observability problem. Observability tells you what an agent did; it is not what stops it.

The habit this is really teaching A leader who cites a verified number and openly refuses to cite an unverified one gets believed on both. The moment you quote a dramatic figure that a sceptic can trace to a thread rather than a postmortem, you have handed them the whole argument.
Decision exercise 1 of 2

Replay 09:14 against your own system ★ 7 min · table discussion

Do this out loud, in pairs, with somebody who knows the code path. The output is not a plan. It is an honest inventory of what you would and would not have seen, which is the only input a2 needs.

Pick one AI feature that a person outside engineering depends on. Name it, name who consumes its output, and name the decision that output feeds. If nobody can name the downstream decision, you have found a different and more interesting problem.

Walk the Beacon chain against it. Is there a data fetch that can time out? Is there a cache or a fallback anywhere in the path? Is there error handling anywhere that swallows a failure and returns something usable instead? Almost every production AI system answers yes three times, because those are all good engineering.

Now the load-bearing question: if that fallback fired at 09:14 this morning, which of your dashboards would have moved? Not which alert would have fired - which pixel anywhere would be different. Write down the honest answer. For most teams it is none.

Finally, time the investigation. If a user reported a wrong answer today with no timestamp and no screenshot, how many hours of whose time would it take to reach the mechanism? That number is your current cost per silent failure, and it is the number to bring to a2.

What to listen for while the room talks Two answers should stop the discussion. "We would see it in the logs" usually means someone would find it if they already knew what to grep for, which is not detection. "The evals would catch it" is the exact claim the Anthropic postmortem disproves for intermittent, absorbed, route-dependent failures - and if it is said in your room, that is the moment to read the sentence in Part 2 out loud.
Decision exercise 2 of 2

Write the rule for "the user says it is wrong and the evals say it is fine" ★ 6 min · write it down

This is the only artifact from today that survives the week. Every organisation resolves this conflict somehow, and the ones that resolve it badly do so by default rather than by decision. Four sentences, agreed in the room, is enough.

The threshold. How many independent user reports about the same AI behaviour, inside what window, constitute a signal that must be investigated even with a green eval suite? Pick an actual number. Two reports in a day is a defensible answer; "when it feels serious" is not.

The owner. Who is accountable for reaching a mechanism, not a hypothesis? A report that gets routed to a triage queue and closed as "cannot reproduce" is the failure mode this rule exists to prevent, and non-reproducibility is expected rather than exculpatory.

The evidence standard. What must a user report capture to be actionable? Minimum viable is a timestamp, the question asked, and the answer received. Agree today that your product surfaces enough to collect those, because retrofitting it during an incident is how a week disappears.

The escalation. What happens when investigation finds nothing because the telemetry does not exist? That outcome must produce an instrumentation ticket rather than a closed report. This is the single sentence that turns today's session into engineering work.

★ The rule, in one line, if you only take one thing A user report about an AI answer is a signal about the system, and "our evals are green" is not a rebuttal to it. It is a statement that the eval set does not contain the failing case - which is new information about the eval set, not reassurance about production.
Homework

Before session a2 - this week ◐ 35-45 min total

  • Read the Anthropic postmortem end to end, and specifically the detection and remediation sections. It is the single most useful forty minutes a technical leader can spend on this subject, because it is a first-party account of instrumentation failing at a company that has no excuse for it.
  • Bring the number from exercise 1, step 4 to the next session: the hours of senior time your last unexplained AI answer consumed. Coverage is a spend decision, and a2 is much shorter if the room already agrees what the current cost of not having it is.
  • Find out whether any of your AI features can currently be asked "what did this specific user see at this specific minute" and answered in under an hour. If the answer is no for all of them, write down which one it should be yes for first, and why that one.
  • Ask your legal or compliance counterpart one question: does our AI assistant's answer count as a statement by us? Moffatt v. Air Canada is a small tribunal award and a large piece of information, and you want their answer before an incident rather than during one.
  • Optional, and the one that changes minds fastest: retell the Beacon story to a non-engineering executive in ninety seconds and watch which part lands. It is usually the green dashboard, not the stale number.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Beacon's p95 latency dashboard stayed green through the entire incident. What is the correct conclusion?

This is the trap that costs teams the most time. Nothing was broken about the measurement, so no amount of retuning it would have helped. The failure lived entirely outside what was being measured, and the fallback path was genuinely faster than the correct path - which means a tighter latency threshold would have made the incident less visible, not more. A monitoring system can only be wrong about things it measures.

2 · Anthropic's postmortem reports that benchmarks "simply didn't capture the degradation users were reporting". Which detail makes that most uncomfortable for your own eval strategy?

All five are real and all five are on the page, but B is the structural one. A resilient system absorbs a fault and still produces an acceptable answer, so a score aggregated over many requests barely moves while individual users are visibly harmed. Resilience and detectability trade against each other, and nobody schedules that trade-off. The platform spread in C carries the other operational lesson: if you multi-home, one platform's telemetry does not represent the others.

3 · In Moffatt v. Air Canada, which signal would actually have caught the failure before the tribunal did?

The response was fast, successful and unexceptional by every conventional measure, which is precisely why the case matters: a hallucination contradicting your own published policy is a legally actionable silent failure. The signal you needed is "bot answer conflicts with source of truth", and almost nobody instruments it - not because it is technically hard, but because it requires somebody to decide what the source of truth is and where it lives. That decision is coverage, and coverage is session a2.
